01Four kinds of counties

Four kinds of counties

Counties sorted by what they faced from 2000 to 2025: fire (1% or more of the county burned), drought (average drought score in the top 25% of counties), both, or neither. Click a group in any chart to highlight its counties.

02Where are we building?

Where are we building?

Each county is colored by two things at once: how fast it built new housing (permits per 1,000 residents per year, in blue) and how much land burned in a county from fires (in orange). The darkest counties are high on both.

How to read the colors: Building levels split all counties into three equal groups by new homes per 1,000 residents per year (low, medium, high). Fire levels show how much of the county burned from 2000 to 2025: none, under 1%, or 1% or more. White counties have no data.

03Growth by group

Fire counties grew fastest

Counties that burned grew about five times faster than unaffected counties and built more housing. Drought-only counties, largely rural farm areas, shrank. Much of this growth is tied to regionalism as people increasinly move West.

How much drought each group lived through

Average weeks each group spent at a drought level or worse, 2000–2025 (about 1,350 weeks in all).

04What causes fires?

People start most fires; lightning burns most land

Fires started by people far outnumber lightning fires, but they are usually caught small. In the West, lightning fires account for most of the acres burned.

05Who starts the fires

Who starts the fires?

Share of fires started by people, early vs. recent years, for counties grouped by how much housing they built. Nationally nearly every fire is human-caused, so look at the West: counties that built more have a higher human-caused share, but the share rose across the board, not only where building boomed.

Counties with at least 10 fires of known cause in each period. Building groups are fifths of all counties by new homes per 1,000 residents.

06Density and fire

Do emptier places burn more?

Each dot is a county. Compare how much land burned with how many fires started: the two measures can tell different stories about sparse and crowded places.

Both axes use a log scale. Counties with no recorded fire sit in the band at the bottom.

07Building after a fire

Do people rebuild, or build more, after a fire?

New homes in the 3 years after a big fire, compared with the 3 years before. The gray line is the rest of the same state over the same years, so a gap between the lines means building changed because of something local, not a statewide boom or slump.

08Every fire, one dot

Every fire, one dot

Each dot is one county-year fire. Higher dots took a bigger share of their state's new homes in the 3 years after the fire than in the 3 years before. Hover for details.

Fire size in the county that year:

Share = the county's new homes divided by all new homes in its state over the same 3 years. Comparing shares removes statewide booms and slumps, and also accounts for a county that was already growing faster than its state before the fire.

09Biggest jumps

Biggest building jumps after a fire

Counties whose share of their state's new homes rose the most after a fire, at the size chosen on the previous pages.

Permits count new homes, including houses rebuilt after being destroyed, but not repairs. The data can't separate a rebuild from a brand-new home, so a jump after a fire could be either. Fires from 2003–2022 so every fire has 3 years before and after. Counties need at least 5 new homes a year before the fire; the table lists counties with 20+. "Rest of state" = counties in the same state with no fire that size, 2000–2025.
